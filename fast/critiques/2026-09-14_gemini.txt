The main issue preventing this from looking like a photo isn't camera optics or stroke density—it's 3D light and geometry physics. Cameras capture light reflecting off inclined, three-dimensional surfaces, whereas this render applies uniform 2D washes and edge outlines.

The core reasons why it looks digital, ranked by impact, along with concrete solutions for each:

1. Zero Directional Lighting / Missing Facets (Highest Cost)

What reads as fake: The foreground mountains are solid dark silhouettes with zero light orientation. Mountains are made of angled planes (lit faces and shadow faces). Because there are no highlights on sun-facing slopes or deep shadows on opposing sides, the mountains look like 2D cardboard cutouts.

Concrete Fix: Model a simple 3D heightmap or directional vector for terrain sloped surfaces. For every mountain region, determine which direction the slope faces relative to the sun. Rays facing the sun get warm directional rim highlights; opposite slopes get deep, cool ambient shadows.

2. High-Frequency Ridge Outlines & "Noise" Edge Artifacts (Single Biggest Single Impact)

What reads as fake: The glowing orange/gold edge outline along the distant ridges and the jagged, saw-tooth outline on the foreground mountains look like digital brush noise or vector outlines. In optics, a sharp distant ridge against a glowing sky does not form a bright golden stroke; it either crisply cuts the sky or bleeds via atmospheric refraction.

Concrete Fix (Impact: Highest Single Jump to Realism): Eliminate edge-tracing strokes entirely. Stop running edge-detection or boundary-drawing routines that lay down outlines along ridge tops. Instead, define mountains strictly by filled shape intersections where light meets shadow, letting the sky-to-land boundary be created purely by the shape's fill rather than a high-frequency boundary stroke.

3. Inverted Atmospheric Scattering & Color Temperature

What reads as fake: The sun is a small white dot high in the sky, but dawn sunlight scatters horizontally across the horizon (Rayleigh/Mie scattering). The sky here is dark violet on the top/sides, pastel blue in the middle, and strange gray patches around the sun. Additionally, the foreground peaks are pitch black while background layers are cyan/purple without a smooth gradient transition.

Concrete Fix: Implement a true radial/exponential atmospheric fade. The sky close to the sun at dawn should be highly saturated gold/orange, transitioning softly into deep blue further away. Every background layer should exponentially blend into the sky color as depth increases, eliminating sharp local color shifts.

4. Flawed Camera Post-Processing Effects

What reads as fake: The simulated camera defects are applied as global 2D overlays that read as filter noise rather than optical physics:

The vignette at the top creates unnatural dark corners that crush the sky.

Chromatic aberration manifests as uniform rainbow bands around the top frame instead of radial lens dispersion.

Veiling glare looks like a dull gray halo rather than volumetric bloom.

Concrete Fix: Turn off artificial post-processing effects until the core geometry and lighting match reality. Replace the hard glare circle with a soft, additive, wide-radius Gaussian bloom centered on the sun light source.

The Single Highest-Impact Change

Replace stroke-based boundary outlines with directional planar shading.

If you strip away the golden ridge-line strokes and render mountain faces with distinct lit sides (facing the dawn sun) and shadowed sides (facing away), the scene will instantly transform from a 2D vector graphic into a photographic 3D landscape.
