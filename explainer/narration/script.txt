This robot isn't from a video model. No camera, no stock art, no AI image generator. Every frame you're looking at was painted into MattPaint, my Paint app, one stroke at a time. About sixty seven million strokes for ten seconds.
Here's how it works. I give Claude Opus one line: a giant robot has knelt in a forest so long that moss grows over it, and the sun breaks through. Opus never draws a picture. It writes the whole world as code. Every armor plate, every rivet, the rust, the moss, the trees, the mist, where the sun sits, where the camera moves.
Then Blender works out the light. It follows the light as it bounces off the metal, through the mist, and between the trees. That's why it looks real. Nobody painted the shadows. The light made them.
Then MattPaint paints it. Watch. Line by line, top to bottom, like a printer. About five hundred ninety thousand strokes for one frame. Then it does it again for the next frame. A hundred and twenty times.
It didn't start this good. The first version used a light engine I built myself. The robot came out as a stack of blocks, and ten seconds took four and a half hours. Moving the light math to Blender made it twenty five times faster. The same shot took seven minutes, and the robot actually looks like a robot.
So is anyone else doing this? I looked. People are having AI write code for animation, and research teams have models writing Blender scenes for still images. There's AI that turns a photo into brush strokes. But a model writing the whole world, real light, and every frame painted into a paint program? I couldn't find anyone else doing that.
Everything runs on my MacBook, except Opus writing the scene. Story Forge, my film studio, adds the music and sound. Both are open source on GitHub. Links are below.
